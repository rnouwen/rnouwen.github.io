Evaluative meaning

Language is rich with evaluative meaning. Why do we say of someone who is very late that they are 'horribly', 'terribly' or 'pretty' late? My view is natural language constantly multitasks, combining objective and subjective information, mixing denotation and connotation. This allows us to efficiently use language to convey very rich ideas about the world, including our own inner world. One example of this is what I call the Goldilocks effect, the fact that we tend to use words with negative valence to express extreme things ('Sue is terribly nice') and words with positive valence for milder judgments ('Sue is pretty nice').

I investigate this mixture of various kinds of meanings in single utterances by looking at exclamatives, adverbs of degree, but also figures of speech like hyperbole.

  1. Hyperbole is not a rational speech act
    Rick Nouwen
    2026
    Under review
    Abs PDF

    This paper concerns what a proper account of hyperbole interpretation should look like. I consider what I will call "rational repair theories" of hyperbole. These are theories that assume that the interpretation of a hyperbolic utterance is triggered by the defective nature of the literal meaning. Because exaggeration makes this literal meaning extremely unlikely to be truthful, the listener reasons that its utility must lie in some other meaning dimension. In particular, I zoom in on the computational approach of Kao et al. 2014, who propose an implementation of this repair idea in the Rational Speech Act framework. I show that there are several problems with this kind of approach and with the idea of rational repair more generally. Ultimately, I suggest that hyperbole may not be a phenomenon that is based on rational pragmatic reasoning at all.
